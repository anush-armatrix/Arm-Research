Autonomous multi-pass arc welding · design reference

Plan, Weld, Rescan

A neural bead model, a search that calls it, and a loop that checks the result. Three components, one measurement holding them together — and one thing the physics refuses to let us do.

01The problem

Filling a deep weld groove takes 20–30 separate passes, each laid on top of the last. Every pass changes the shape the next one lands on. Get one slightly wrong and the error doesn't sit still — the next pass builds on it.

The classical way to predict a weld is finite-element simulation, far too slow for a control loop. So we train a fast stand-in — and then face the question that ruins most fast stand-ins: what happens when you feed it its own output thirty times?

02Three components, three jobs

The most useful decision in this design is keeping these separate. A bad weld could come from any of the three — and if they're one component, you can't tell which.

Learned · a network

f — the bead model

“With these settings, on this surface, what bead do I get?” Replaces the slow physics. Microseconds per answer, which is what makes searching possible at all.

in: surface + settings → out: 86 numbers

Ordinary code · a search

The planner

“What settings should I use?” Proposes about a thousand candidate plans, asks f about each, discards the illegal ones, keeps the best, refines.

in: measured surface → out: 5-pass plan, 1 committed

Ordinary code · a state machine

The controller

“What actually happened, and what does it mean?” Scans the real weld, subtracts the prediction, classifies the difference, and decides whether to continue, warn, or stop.

in: scan + prediction → out: replan, or a fault

03Inside f: two kinds of input

The groove is a shape — 161 height readings in a row, where neighbours matter. The machine settings are eight unrelated numbers. Push both through the same layers and you throw away the fact that the shape is a shape.

So each gets its own treatment and they meet at the end. The settings also reach across to gate the geometry: weld hot and slow and a wide span of surrounding metal is relevant; weld cold and fast and only the metal at the arc is. Stating that coupling directly is cheaper than making the network discover it through depth.

04The idea the whole design rests on

Two broken watches. One randomly gains or loses a few seconds an hour — after a week it's still roughly right, because the errors cancel. The other gains exactly two seconds every hour — after a week it's six minutes out.

Same error per hour. Wildly different after a week. Welding is the second watch. If the model predicts beads a hair too tall every time, pass thirty sits on twenty-nine accumulated hairs.

The trap: the standard way to score a model — squared error — punishes the size of mistakes and is blind to their direction. A model that is always +0.1 mm scores identically to one that is randomly ±0.1 mm. Nothing discourages the watch that always gains.

Consequence for training

The model is trained by laying five beads in a row, each on its own previous answer, and scoring three things at once: how big the mistakes are, whether they lean one way, and how far off it is after all five. All three fall out of the same run — the last two are numbers a normal trainer computes and discards.

06Inside the planner

The planner has no learned parts. It is a sampler, a batched simulator, a filter and a weighted average — run four times over. Every step is inspectable, which is the whole reason it isn't a network.

It does not solve for the best plan analytically. It guesses a thousand plans, simulates all of them at once, throws away the illegal ones, and moves its guessing toward whatever survived best. Then repeats. That is the entire algorithm.

Why masks and not penalties

A soft penalty can always be outweighed. If a 92° toe angle merely costs something, a plan that fills the groove beautifully will buy its way past it — and you will have optimised your way into a lack-of-fusion defect. Feasibility is not a preference.

6.3 · How the search moves

Cross-entropy method: keep a rough guess, sample around it, keep what worked, move the guess. No gradients — which matters, because the constraints are hard rejections and the cost surface over torch placement is multi-modal (a bead can go left or right of a ridge, and a gradient picks neither).

6.4 · Where the 5 comes from, arithmetically

Section 05 gave the number. Here is the algebra behind it, because it is three lines and it is the only place in the design where a horizon is derived rather than chosen.

why the horizon is finite over H passes, per-pass error e has mean μ and standard deviation σ consistent part Σ eh ≈ H · μ adds up — every pass leans the same way random part sd( Σ eh ) = √H · σ partially cancels they are equal when H · μ = √H · σ ⇒ H* = ( σ / μ )² measured on a real 31-pass weld: H* = 12.3 ⇒ σ/μ = 3.51 i.e. a 0.1 mm/pass bias is as damaging by pass 12 as 0.351 mm/pass of noise rescan before H* and the consistent part never gets to win → K = H = rescan interval = 5, comfortably short of 12.3

6.5 · What the guess actually is

Stage A says “a centre + spread for each of 5 passes × 4 knobs”. That is a Gaussian, and it is worth being exact, because it is twenty numbers and twenty spreads — nothing more.

Five passes × four knobs is 20 dimensions. The distribution carries 20 means and 20 standard deviations, and no correlations between them. It is diagonal: each knob is drawn independently of every other.

  • The means start from the existing placement heuristic — left, right, centre, climbing as the groove fills — because starting from real practice beats starting from nothing.
  • The spreads come from the welding procedure’s own latitude, set so ±2σ spans what the procedure permits. The search box is the training box: no plan can wander somewhere f has never seen.
  • Diagonal rather than full, for two reasons. A full 20×20 covariance is 210 numbers estimated from about 102 survivors — badly under-determined. And consecutive passes were measured to be independent, so there is no pass-to-pass correlation to capture anyway.
the whole loop q(a) = Normal( μ , diag(σ²) ) 20 means, 20 spreads draw 1024 → score each → keep the best 10% → refit μ and σ to those μ ← (1−α)·μ + α·mean(elites) α = 0.7 σ ← (1−α)·σ + α·sd(elites) repeat 4 rounds, then commit the first action only

6.6 · Measured: the budget split is the defect, not the algorithm

A literature review and an ablation, run 2026‑09‑12, changed what this section recommends. Three findings, in increasing order of how much they matter.

These algorithms are one family, not rivals. The Information-Geometric Optimization framework shows CEM, CMA‑ES, natural evolution strategies and estimation-of- distribution algorithms are the same flow under different selection schemes — a weight as a function of rank. CEM is recovered exactly by a hard cut at the elite fraction. CMA‑ES’s default weights are already a soft weighting over ranks, which matters here: reading ranks rather than values makes the search invariant to any monotone distortion of the objective, and a biased f is exactly such a distortion.

A diagonal cannot align to a rotated valley. And this valley is rotated, by construction: deposited area is η·k·I / v_t, so current and travel speed matter mostly through their ratio. That is a diagonal direction in the box the sampler draws from.

But the biggest defect is not the shape of the distribution — it is how the budget is spent. Adapting a covariance needs iterations, not samples. Four updates cannot learn a 20×20 covariance however many samples each one sees. Measured on a randomly rotated ill-conditioned valley, holding the total budget fixed at 4,096 evaluations:

The correction

There is an interior optimum, not a monotone trend: 12 × 341 is worse than the current design, because three survivors cannot estimate twenty spreads. So the fix is not “more rounds” — it is balance, and the largest single improvement available to the planner costs nothing and changes no algorithm. A hybrid combining CMA‑ES, MPPI and mixture models was investigated and rejected: two of the proposed combinations turn out to be redundant with CMA‑ES, one actively forfeits the invariance that protects against a biased f, and restart schemes need about 2×107 evaluations at this dimension against a budget of 4,000.

07Where the physics actually lives

This is not a physics-informed network in the usual sense — there is no equation in the loss, and the brief rules that approach out. The physics is in four other places instead, and they are not equally strong.

The rule: put each constraint at the lowest level where it can be made exact. If you can calculate it, calculate it. If you can check it, check it and reject. Only put it in a loss when you can do neither.

StrengthMechanismExamplesHolds when?
1 · structuralin the data path — cannot be violated area enforced by the rebuild · wire feed derived from current · toe angles as boundary conditions always, no training needed
2 · the labelsthe answers came out of a physics solver surface-energy minimisation · the thermal field on the training distribution only
3 · maskshard rejection in the search edge angle < 90° · the machine envelope always — cannot be outbid
4 · hyperparametersphysics fixes the architecture's shape K = H = rescan = 5 · 25 mm stations · the search spread by construction

Level 4 is the easiest to overlook and arguably the deepest: none of those numbers were chosen. They came out of measurements.

7.1 · One change: calculate the metal, don't predict it

The rebuild currently scales the bead so its area matches what the model predicted. That is self-consistency, not mass conservation — predict the wrong area and the rebuild faithfully enforces the wrong area.

But the metal delivered is arithmetic: wire fed in, divided by travel speed. The planner already knows both — it chose them. The code that computes it describes itself as "the one hard invariant in the stack."

The fix, and a free bonus

Override the predicted area with the computed one before rebuilding. Mass conservation becomes exact against the wire, and the model has one fewer thing to get wrong — it only ever has to predict shape. And keep the area output anyway: predicted-versus-calculated then becomes a free self-test that fires on every single inference, rather than a surprise at the end of a rollout.

7.2 · Three costs the planner should have and doesn't

All three are already computed for every pass. None is used.

7.4 · And a correction to this document

§6.2 previously listed a limit on how much settings may change between passes, justified by the 26 mm pool. That was wrong. Between passes the metal has frozen and there is a cooling wait, so consecutive passes may differ freely — root, fill and cap passes routinely do.

Measured in our own data: consecutive passes jump by 1.4× the full legal latitude on current, and 3.3× on travel speed. The model was trained with no such limit, so a planner constraint enforcing one would contradict its own training data.

The limit is real, but it belongs to the within-pass adjustment of §9.1 — the fast loop — where the pool genuinely does average commands over its own length.

08The thing the physics won't allow

The obvious design is a controller that watches the bead form and corrects on the fly. It cannot be built, and the reason is a number.

The arc does not leave solid metal behind it. It leaves a pool of liquid about 26 mm long, almost all of it behind the torch. The metal just laid is still moving, still settling, still deciding its shape.

What this rules in and out

Correcting the groove ahead of you: yes. It is solid, already its final shape, and you see it seconds early. Correcting the bead behind you: no. Not merely late or imprecise — impossible, because the pool averages any command over its own 26 mm. Corrections happen between passes or ahead of the torch, never on the metal just deposited.

09How the controller works

Here is the part that surprises people: the controller never computes a correction. It plans again from what it sees.

Thermal distortion is the classic worry — the plate warps as it heats, so the groove you are filling is not the groove in the drawing. Nothing in this system predicts that, and nothing needs to. The warping has already happened by the time you scan, so it is baked into the measurement. Plan from the measurement and it is handled.

9.1 · Two timescales, two different jobs

The controller runs at two rates, and they do genuinely different things. Confusing them is the most common way this kind of design goes wrong.

9.2 · The one signal it works from

Everything the controller does comes from a single subtraction: what the scanner saw minus what f predicted. Call it the residual. Its shape is what carries the information — not its size.

9.4 · When nothing legal will work

The last row of that table is the one most often left out of designs like this, and the most dangerous to omit. If the search finds that no setting inside the machine's qualified envelope closes the deficit, the controller must not quietly pick the least-bad option. It climbs a ladder instead, and every rung is a bigger admission.

Widen the search, still legally

Try a longer horizon, or allow a placement the heuristic would not have proposed. Cheap, invisible, no consequences.

Add a pass

The layer needs more metal than the plan allowed. Costs time, changes nothing else.

Re-cut the layer plan

The remaining volume no longer divides the way it was going to. Replan the whole layer stack from the measured groove, not just the next few passes.

Stop and ask a human

The groove is outside anything the qualified procedure covers — warped past tolerance, or a defect that needs grinding out. This is a success, not a failure: the alternative is welding confidently into a joint that cannot be made sound.

9.5 · Does the loop actually close in time?

Worth checking, because a controller that cannot finish thinking before the next pass starts is not a controller. The numbers are comfortable, and not for the reason people expect.

The useful conclusion

The controller is not compute-limited. It is limited by measurement and by how fast steel cools. That means there is no reason to compromise the search to make it fast — and it means the design's real constraints are physical, which is a much better place to be than fighting a deadline.

9.6 · What the controller does not do

  • It does not touch the bead being laid. §08 — the pool makes that impossible, not merely hard.
  • It does not model distortion. It measures the consequence and replans. This is the design's central simplification and the reason it can work at all.
  • It does not control penetration or seam tracking. Those are separate, faster loops with their own sensors — deliberately out of scope here.
  • It does not adjust torch angle. There is no torch-angle term in the physics at all, by an explicit earlier decision: adding an input the model cannot respond to would be worse than leaving it out, because it would look supported.

The result that matters

Section 05 argued that a consistent error is what kills a multi-pass build, because it accumulates while random error partly cancels. That is now measured on our own simulator: a one-off disturbance decays over the following passes, while a sustained bias compounds as roughly N0.67. So bias is the number to watch — and it came out at −0.005 mm, a fiftyfold reduction from where training started. An unbiased f at 0.8 mm beats a biased one at 0.3 mm.

Tellingly, the largest single improvement of the whole exercise was not architectural. It was changing which run gets selected — carrying bias in the early-stopping criterion rather than magnitude alone. That one line moved bias six times closer to zero and five times tighter across seeds.

The part that does not work

The toe angles are barely better than a constant — 12.31° against 12.63° for always predicting the same number — and they gate the hard 90° lack-of-fusion limit, so this is the failure that matters. Three fixes were built and measured, and all three were refuted: weighting the angles in the loss (inert across a 50× sweep), replacing the pooling that discards where a feature occurred (inert across three seeds), and a direct linear path to the outputs (buys 0.3°, costs a thirtyfold worse bias).

Two things explain it. A quarter of the variance is unreachable by construction: 26% is driven by quantities the model is deliberately forbidden to see, because they are unobservable when a plan is made. And of what remains, a 32-coefficient linear regression beats the 47,158-parameter network — 11.06° against 12.31° — which points at the two angle outputs being starved by the eighty-four others sharing the same trunk.

The practical answer is not a better prediction. It is to treat the measured ~12° of uncertainty as a safety margin on the 90° limit, which is what a hard constraint is for.

The honest risk

The paper this architecture follows trained on 592,737 cross-sections. We have 6,163 — about 1%. So the network is deliberately built at roughly a quarter of the published size, and the part of their design needing the most data (a pre-trained attention module) is dropped because we have no labels for it. That is the likeliest place this underperforms its source, and it should be the first thing measured rather than hoped about.
