Two Ways to Plan a Weld

Both pipelines share the bead model, the representation, the simulators and the controller. They fork at one question — what does the search enumerate? — and that single difference decides how each one fails.

There are two readings of the planning problem. One searches over the knobs and checks what comes out. The other draws the finished weld first and solves backwards for the knobs. They are often discussed as rival architectures. They are not: they are the same machinery with the search pointed at different things.

01Action-first — search the settings

Plainly: you have four knobs. Guess a sequence of settings, ask the bead model what weld that produces, score how well the groove ends up filled, and keep guessing better. Like finding a radio station by turning the dial and listening after each turn.

Formally: the search enumerates action sequences {V_k, x_TCP,k}, evaluated by rolling f forward over a horizon and scoring the accumulated fill. CEM or MPPI resamples the distribution toward the better-scoring candidates.

02Target-first — search the geometry

Plainly: draw the finished weld first. Decide how many layers, how many beads per layer, and what each bead should look like. Then work out what settings produce each drawn bead. Like drawing a tile layout on paper before cutting any tiles — the drawing is cheap and can be redrawn a thousand times.

Formally: the search enumerates fill keys. A fill key is a 1-D array whose position is the layer index and whose value is the bead count in that layer, so [2, 3, 3] means two beads in layer one and three in each of layers two and three. Candidates are scored by intersection-over-union against sampled cross-sections, then the winner's per-pass targets are inverted: V_k = f⁻¹(Δs*_k ; s_k).

03The fork, and where they rejoin

Drawn together, the shared spine is obvious. One scan goes in; one committed action comes out; the weld-and-rescan loop is identical. Only the middle differs.

05The asymmetry that decides the ordering

Everything in that table is a trade — cost against coverage, maturity against generality. One row is not a trade.

Action-first reads f's values. Target-first steers by f's derivatives. Differentiating a model is a strictly stronger demand than evaluating it, and the two failure modes are not comparable in severity.

Action-first, with a biased f

The bead estimate is consistently wrong. The controller measures the real surface every pass and attributes the residual, so bias appears as a correction signal and gets absorbed.

Measured: a one-off disturbance decays, 0.070 → 0.036 mm over eight passes.

Target-first, with a biased f

The gradient field tilts. The solver walks confidently to the wrong settings and reports a low loss doing it.

Surrogate bias, unlike variance, does not average away with more samples — so more search does not rescue it.

Measured in-house, 17 Sep 2026

22 of 36 trained checkpoints cannot see the groove at all. Flattening a real groove to a flat plate moves their prediction by less than 0.01 mm, against a ~1 mm per-pass spec. They fit to a plausible ~1.2 mm height RMSE using 8 of 169 inputs. Plain ReLU produced this in 20 of 20 runs, via a dying-ReLU cascade that severs the geometry branch.

A surface-blind f has identically zero gradient with respect to the groove. Action-first still limps, because it perturbs actions and rescans. Target-first would return the same settings for every groove and score itself well while doing it.

This is why the branch audit is a prerequisite for target-first, not a side quest — and why the chosen ordering builds the fill planner first and the gradient solver second.

06What each buys that the other cannot

Action-first wins on robustness and maturity

It tolerates a mediocre f, most of it exists, and its failure mode is visible in the residual. It needs no inverse solver, no fill-key enumerator, no crown parameterisation.

Target-first wins on two things that cannot be bought otherwise

  • Whole-groove awareness is structural. Action-first sees only its horizon. A plan that fills beautifully for five passes and strands the last two against a wall is invisible until it happens.
  • Pass count becomes a decision. In action-first it is whatever the search produces. The patent's tiebreaker — equal bead count, fewer layers wins — encodes a real process preference, since arc starts and stops are where defects concentrate.

Can action-first borrow the missing piece?

Half of it. A cheap geometric pre-score is easy to bolt on and directly attacks the ~10³ call count. Whole-groove awareness is not transferable: the natural fix is extending the horizon to the whole build, and that is exactly what the rollout arithmetic forbids. Error compounds, K = H = 5 came from a measured bias/variance crossover, and the plant is open-loop unstable. You cannot plan 31 passes by rolling f forward and expect the last one to mean anything.

Read the drift number carefully

Drift is small — but f's single-step error is already at the spec, and the drift sample thins to n = 4 builds by pass 8, because the test split holds few long builds. Low drift over ten passes is real; it is not yet evidence about thirty-one.

08Open decisions

Two target-first decisions were taken and have no code behind them: the asymmetric V-groove and the parameterised crown. GrooveSpec is still three numbers and symmetric, and the 850-build corpus assumes it — widening the groove description invalidates that corpus for any model that must generalise across wall angles.

One decision was never made at all: what the fill planner optimises. Fewest passes within the area envelope, a fixed heat-input budget per pass, or no objective with the solver doing all the optimising. It determines whether pass count is an output of the search or an input to it, so the planner cannot be specified until it is settled.
